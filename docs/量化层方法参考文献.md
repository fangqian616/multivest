## 1. Volatility-Managed Portfolios — *both sides*

### 1a. The original claim

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Volatility-Managed Portfolios |
| **Authors** | Alan Moreira, Tyler Muir |
| **Year** | 2017 (NBER WP 22208, 2016) |
| **Venue** | *Journal of Finance*, 72(4), 1611–1644. doi:10.1111/jofi.12513. JF is the top-3 empirical asset-pricing journal (with JFE and RFS); this is a "field-defining, heavily cited" placement. |
| **NBER WP (free)** | https://www.nber.org/papers/w22208 · PDF: http://www.nber.org/papers/w22208.pdf |
| **Publisher** | https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12513 |

**The specification (this is the exact object you asked for).** Let $f_{t+1}$ be a factor's monthly excess
return and let $c$ be a scaling constant. The managed portfolio is

```
f~_{t+1} = ( c / σ̂²_t(f) ) · f_{t+1}                                        ... (MM eq. 1)
```

with the proxy for conditional variance being **last month's realized variance computed from daily returns
inside that month**:

```
σ̂²_t(f) = RV²_t(f) = (1/22) · Σ_{d=1..22} ( f_{t+d} − (1/22)·Σ_{d=1..22} f_{t+d} )²     ... (MM eq. 2)
```

Note the precise structure: it is the **sample variance of the ~22 daily returns within month $t$**, *not*
the squared monthly return. This matters enormously — a single squared monthly return is an absurdly noisy
variance proxy and will destroy the strategy.

The constant $c$ is chosen **ex post** so that the managed portfolio has the *same unconditional standard
deviation* as the buy-and-hold portfolio (their footnote 5). Because $c$ is a pure scale factor it does
**not** affect the Sharpe ratio — that is why using the full sample to set $c$ is not a look-ahead
contamination. Your implementation should do exactly this.

**The predictive regression** they run is the test of whether volatility timing adds anything:

```
f~_{t+1} = α + β · f_{t+1} + ε_{t+1}                                          ... (MM eq. 3)
```

A positive, significant $\alpha$ means the scaled portfolio spans a wider mean-variance frontier than the
original factor. They also report the **appraisal ratio** α/σ_ε and the implied Sharpe gain:

```
SR_new = sqrt( SR_old² + (α/σ_ε)² )                                           ... (MM §2.4)
Percentage utility gain: ΔU_MV(%) = ( SR_new² − SR_old² ) / SR_old²           ... (MM eq. 4)
```

**Reported numbers** (their Table 1, Panel A, sample 1926–2015 for Mkt/SMB/HML/Mom; factors annualized by
×12; heteroskedasticity-adjusted SEs in parentheses):

| Factor | β | α (%/yr) | (SE) | R² | RMSE | Appraisal ratio α/σ_ε |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mkt | 0.61 (0.05) | **4.86** | (1.56) | 0.37 | 51.39 | **0.34** |
| SMB | 0.62 (0.08) | −0.58 | (0.91) | 0.38 | 30.44 | — |
| HML | 0.57 (0.07) | 1.97 | (1.02) | 0.32 | 34.92 | 0.20 |
| **Mom** | 0.47 (0.07) | **12.51** | (1.71) | 0.22 | 50.37 | **0.875** |
| RMW | 0.62 (0.08) | 2.44 | (0.83) | 0.38 | 20.16 | 0.41 |
| CMA | 0.68 (0.05) | 0.38 | (0.67) | 0.46 | 17.55 | — |
| FX Carry | 0.71 (0.08) | 2.78 | (1.49) | 0.33 | 25.34 | 0.44 |
| ROE | 0.63 (0.07) | 5.48 | (0.97) | 0.40 | 23.69 | 0.80 |
| IA | 0.68 (0.05) | 1.55 | (0.67) | 0.47 | 16.58 | 0.32 |

The market alpha of 4.86%/yr with SE 1.56 gives **t ≈ 3.1**. Headline claim (abstract): "an overall **25%
increase in the buy-and-hold Sharpe ratio**" for the market, and a "$1 → $20,000 vs $4,000" cumulative
comparison 1926–2015. Note the striking pattern in the table: the **beta is well below 1 in every case**
(0.47–0.71). The strategy is not a scaling of the factor; it is a *different* portfolio that is
systematically under-exposed on average and correctly repositioned across volatility states.

**What it buys you.** A parameter-free, real-time-implementable risk-timing overlay that needs only a
variance estimate. It dovetails perfectly with your existing HAR model: replace `RV²_t` in eq. 2 with your
HAR one-step forecast and you have a *forecast-based* volatility-managed portfolio, which is exactly what
MM do in their Appendix A.1.

### 1b. The dispute — you asked for both sides, so here are three separate attacks

**Attack 1 — out-of-sample failure (the strongest and most important for you).**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | On the performance of volatility-managed portfolios |
| **Authors** | Scott Cederburg, Michael S. O'Doherty, Feifei Wang, Xuemin (Sterling) Yan |
| **Year** | 2020 |
| **Venue** | ***Journal of Financial Economics***, 138(1), 95–117. doi:10.1016/j.jfineco.2020.04.015 — **not** the Journal of Finance. |
| **Link** | https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2020.04.015 · AEA/SSRN: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3204905 |

Their finding: the in-sample alphas are real but **do not survive out-of-sample**. Using real-time,
recursively-estimated scaling decisions, the volatility-managed strategies deliver **Sharpe ratios
statistically indistinguishable from — and often lower than — the unmanaged factors**, and the in-sample
α's largely reflect the ex-post scaling constant and the specific sample. For the market factor in
particular the OOS evidence is weak.

**Attack 2 — transaction costs.**

Barroso & Detzel (2021), "Do limits to arbitrage explain the benefits of volatility-managed portfolios?",
*Journal of Financial Economics* 140(3), 744–767, doi:10.1016/j.jfineco.2021.02.009. Volatility management
generates substantial turnover (you are rebalancing 12× a year on a signal that swings a lot), and
net-of-cost performance deteriorates materially. They tie the residual profitability to limits to arbitrage.

**Attack 3 — the reconciliation, and it is the most useful paper of the three for your design.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | A Multifactor Perspective on Volatility-Managed Portfolios |
| **Authors** | Victor DeMiguel, Alberto Martín-Utrera, Raman Uppal |
| **Year** | 2024 |
| **Venue** | *Journal of Finance*, 79(6), doi:10.1111/jofi.13395 (forthcoming/2024). |
| **Free PDF** | https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/3716/1/The%20Journal%20of%20Finance%20-%202024%20-%20DeMIGUEL%20-%20A%20Multifactor%20Perspective%20on%20Volatility%E2%80%90Managed%20Portfolios.pdf |

Their abstract is unambiguous: *"Cederburg et al. show that these strategies fail out-of-sample, and
Barroso and Detzel show they do not survive transaction costs. We propose a **conditional multifactor
portfolio** that outperforms its unconditional counterpart even out-of-sample and net of costs."* They
report an out-of-sample, net-of-costs Sharpe ratio **13% higher** than the unconditional counterpart. The
key design change: manage the **multifactor combination** conditionally, not each factor marginally.

**The honest synthesis for your tool.** Do **not** market "volatility-managed portfolios give +25%
Sharpe" as a settled result. Market it as: *"volatility scaling is a risk-management overlay whose
in-sample alpha is well documented and whose out-of-sample, net-of-cost benefit is contested; the
evidence favours applying it to a diversified combination rather than to single assets."* Your 2018–2026
sample is short and covers exactly one volatility regime transition (COVID 2020, 2022 rates shock), so
your own OOS estimate will be noisy. Report the block-bootstrap CI around the Sharpe difference — you
already have that machinery.

**Implementation notes / gotchas.**

1. **Use within-month daily variance, never squared monthly returns.** (Eq. 2 above.)
2. **Set $c$ ex post for equal unconditional vol.** It is Sharpe-neutral and makes the comparison clean.
3. **Volatility of volatility is the whole mechanism.** The strategy makes money because lagged variance
   forecasts current variance but *not* returns. Sanity-check this on your data *first*: regress next-day
   (or next-month) return on lagged variance. If the coefficient is significantly positive on your sample,
   volatility management will hurt. MM's claim is that it is ≈ 0.
4. **Do not scale by forecast volatility and also de-mean it.** MM scale by inverse variance, i.e. the
   weight is 1/σ², not 1/σ. Using 1/σ is a different (and weaker) strategy. Be deliberate about which you
   implement and label it correctly.
5. **Leverage blow-up.** 1/σ² weights explode when σ̂² is near zero. Winsorize the weight at, say, the
   1st/99th percentile of 1/σ̂², or cap gross leverage at 2–3×. This is the single most common way these
   implementations blow up in practice, and it will absolutely happen on a low-volatility asset.
6. **Turnover accounting.** Report one-way turnover; expect ~2–3×/yr on a monthly rebalance. If your tool
   charges no costs, add a bps-per-turnover switch so users see the Barroso–Detzel effect directly.

## 2. Model Confidence Set (MCS) — the correct way to say "my HAR beats the alternatives"

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | The Model Confidence Set |
| **Authors** | Peter Reinhard Hansen, Asger Lunde, James M. Nason |
| **Year** | 2011 |
| **Venue** | ***Econometrica***, 79(2), 453–497. doi:10.3982/ECTA5771. Econometrica is the top theory/econometrics journal; this paper has ~2,100 citations and the MCS is now standard practice for forecast comparison. |
| **Link** | https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA5771 |
| **Free full algorithm** | Hansen, Lunde & Nason (2003), "Choosing the Best Volatility Models: The Model Confidence Set Approach", Brown University WP 2003-05 → https://economics.brown.edu/sites/g/files/dprerj726/files/papers/2003-5_paper.pdf (this WP contains the complete Appendix A/B with every formula and the algorithm; the 2011 Econometrica paper is the canonical citation but is paywalled) |

### Setup

Models indexed $i = 1,\dots,m$. Model $i$'s loss at time $t$: $L_{i,t}$. Loss **differential**:

```
d_{ij,t} = L_{i,t} − L_{j,t},     i, j = 1..m,  t = 1..n
```

Sample averages: `d̄_ij = (1/n) Σ_t d_{ij,t}`, and model $i$'s performance relative to the model set:

```
d̄_i· = (1/(m−1)) Σ_{j∈M} d̄_ij        (2003 WP writes 1/m; the published version uses 1/(m−1))
```

**Set of superior models** (the thing you are trying to estimate):

```
M* = { i ∈ M₀ : E(d_{ij,t}) ≤ 0  for all j ∈ M₀ }
```

The MCS $\hat{M}^*_α$ is a random set with $P(M^* ⊆ \hat{M}^*_α) ≥ 1 − α$.

### The two EPA test statistics

The null is **Equal Predictive Ability across the set $M$**: $H_0: E(d_{ij,t}) = 0$ for all $i,j ∈ M$.

**Range statistic:**

```
T_R = max_{i,j ∈ M}  | d̄_ij | / sqrt( vâr(d̄_ij) )
```

**Semi-quadratic statistic:**

```
T_SQ = Σ_{ i<j, i,j ∈ M }  ( d̄_ij )² / vâr(d̄_ij)
```

Both are functions of the same estimated variance matrix; $T_{SQ}$ is the less conservative of the two and
is what you should use (HLN's simulations show $T_R$ puts more models in the set on average, and the
Bonferroni bound is far worse than either — their Table 7 shows the Bonferroni test "lacks power so λ needs
to be very large before the Bonferroni method is able to tell good and bad models apart").

### Elimination rule

```
Model to eliminate:   i† = arg max_{i ∈ M}  d̄_i· / sqrt( vâr(d̄_i·) )
```

i.e. the model whose performance is worst **relative to the average of the surviving set**, standardized by
the bootstrap standard error of that relative performance. Note the subtlety that a *bad* model can survive:
a high relative variance (low correlation of its loss with the average loss) keeps it in.
HLN give the decomposition
`var(d̄_i·) = var(L̄_i·)·(1 + var(L̄_i·)/var(L̄_·,·) − 2·sqrt(var(L̄_i·)/var(L̄_·,·))·corr(L̄_i·, L̄_·,·))`
and note that their NAGARCH22 survived *because* its correlation with the average loss was only 0.964 vs.
>0.99 for most models.

### Algorithm (Appendix B.1)

```
1. M = M₀  (all candidate models)
2. Test EPA on M:
   a. If NOT rejected → M̂*_α = M, stop. Report M as the (1−α) model confidence set.
   b. If rejected:
      i.   compute d̄_i· for all i ∈ M
      ii.  i† = arg max_i  d̄_i· / sqrt(vâr(d̄_i·))
      iii. remove i† from M;  go to 2
```

### Bootstrap implementation (Appendix B — this is the part that is easy to get wrong)

**Block bootstrap, not iid.** HLN's Assumption 2 requires geometric strong mixing; loss differentials are
serially dependent (volatility clustering guarantees this), so iid resampling understates variance.

```
STEP 1 — resamples
  (a) Estimate the block length k: fit an AR(p) to each d_{ij,t}, pick p by information criterion,
      set k = max over (i,j) of the selected lag length.
  (b) v = smallest integer ≥ n/k   (number of blocks)
  (c) For each resample b = 1..B:
        for a = 0..v−1:  τ^b_{a·k+1} ~ iid Uniform{1..n}     (first element of each block)
        for t = 2..k:    τ^b_{a·k+t} = τ^b_{a·k+t−1} + 1  (mod n)   (wrap around)
      → resample index vector (τ^b_1, ..., τ^b_n)   (truncate to length n)

STEP 2 — variance estimates from the resamples
  d̄*_{b,ij} = (1/n) Σ_{t=1..n} d_{ij, τ^b_t}
  vâr(d̄_ij) = (1/B) Σ_{b=1..B} ( d̄*_{b,ij} − d̄_ij )²
  d̄*_{b,i·} = (1/(m−1)) Σ_{j∈M} d̄*_{b,ij}
  vâr(d̄_i·) = (1/B) Σ_{b=1..B} ( d̄*_{b,i·} − d̄_i· )²

STEP 3 — bootstrap null distribution of the statistic (IMPORTANT: centre on d̄, not on 0)
  T*_{b,R}  = max_{i,j ∈ M}  | d̄*_{b,ij} − d̄_ij | / sqrt( vâr(d̄_ij) )
  T*_{b,SQ} = Σ_{i<j ∈ M}    ( d̄*_{b,ij} − d̄_ij )² / vâr(d̄_ij)

  p̂_R  = (1/B) Σ_b 1{ T*_{b,R}  > T_R  }
  p̂_SQ = (1/B) Σ_b 1{ T*_{b,SQ} > T_SQ }
```

The **centring by $\bar d_{ij}$** in Step 3 is what imposes the null. This is the standard step people skip
and it makes the test wrong (it becomes a test that the losses are zero, not that they are equal).

**MCS p-values** (a very useful by-product — one p-value per model):

```
Index the m−1 EPA tests by k = 1..m−1 with p-value p_(k).
κ(i) = the index of the test that eliminated model i.
Convention: κ(i*) = m and p_(m) = 1 for the surviving best model.
MCS p-value:   p̂_i = max_{ k ≤ κ(i) } p_(k)

Then:  i ∈ M̂*_α  ⟺  α ≤ p̂_i
```

So you get a ranking with a p-value per model and the MCS at any α is just a threshold. HLN used
$B = 1{,}000$ resamples in their simulations.

### Applying it to your volatility models under QLIKE

Your HAR model already uses QLIKE loss. QLIKE is

```
L( h_t, RV_t ) = RV_t/h_t − log(RV_t/h_t) − 1
```

where $h_t$ is the forecast variance and $RV_t$ the proxy. It is **robust to noise in the volatility proxy**
(Patton 2011, *Journal of Econometrics* 160(1), 246–256, doi:10.1016/j.jeconom.2010.03.034 proves QLIKE is
one of the loss functions whose ranking is preserved under a noisy but conditionally unbiased proxy — this
is exactly your situation, since on OHLC-only data *every* RV proxy you have is noisy). Concretely:

1. Build candidate set $M_0$ = your existing HAR, HAR-RV-J, HAR-RV-CJ, HARQ, plus the range-based HARs
   from items 10/11, plus a random-walk and a constant-variance benchmark.
2. $L_{i,t}$ = QLIKE of model $i$'s one-step-ahead forecast at $t$.
3. Run the MCS at α = 0.05 and 0.10, report the MCS under both $T_R$ and $T_{SQ}$.
4. Report the MCS p-values as a table — "HARQ is in the 90% MCS, HAR-RV-J is not" is a much stronger and
   more honest statement than "HARQ has lower QLIKE", and it directly answers the "is this difference real
   given that the models are near-nested and highly correlated?" question.

**Implementation notes / gotchas.**

1. **$m(m−1)/2$ differential series.** For $m = 8$ models that's 28 series. Precompute the full $d_{ij}$
   matrix once ($n × 28$), then every bootstrap resample is index arithmetic on that matrix. Pure Python,
   no linear algebra needed.
2. **Block length.** The AR-based selection in the appendix is fiddly. A defensible short cut is
   $k \approx \lceil n^{1/3} \rceil$ or $k = 20$ for daily data with ~2,000 observations. Report the choice
   and show robustness to $k \in \{10, 20, 40\}$ — the MCS is usually not very sensitive.
3. **`vâr(d̄_ij)` can hit zero** for pairs of models that are numerically identical (e.g. two parameterizations
   that collapse to the same fit). Guard the division, and drop exactly-duplicate models from $M_0$ up front.
4. **Use the same $B$ and the same resample indices across the whole sequential procedure.** Re-drawing
   fresh indices at each elimination step makes the p-values non-monotone and the MCS p-values incoherent.
   Note HLN's phrase "the bootstrap is used repeatedly in the sequential testing" — reused, not redrawn.
5. **$T_R$ satisfies the "all superior models are in the MCS with frequency ≥ 1−α" property more robustly;
   $T_{SQ}$ is more powerful but deletes superior models at a higher rate**, especially as $m$ grows. If you
   report one number, report $T_{SQ}$ and note the trade-off.
6. **Cost.** $B = 1{,}000$ × ($m$−1) elimination steps × 28 series is on the order of 10⁷ Python operations.
   In CPython that's seconds-to-a-minute, acceptable for an offline tool. Precompute the QLIKE loss **levels**
   $L_{i,t}$ once, then the resampled $\bar d^*$ is a mean over a gathered list — no recomputation of QLIKE.

## 3. Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP) — the one that actually fits a no-numpy codebase

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Building Diversified Portfolios that Outperform Out of Sample |
| **Authors** | Marcos López de Prado |
| **Year** | 2016 |
| **Venue** | ***The Journal of Portfolio Management***, 42(4), 59–69. doi:10.3905/jpm.2016.42.4.059. JPM is the leading practitioner journal in asset management — lower academic prestige than JF/JFE but high practitioner credibility, which is the right register for a portfolio-construction method. |
| **Link** | https://doi.org/10.3905/jpm.2016.42.4.059 · SSRN free version: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.2708678 (SSRN is unreachable from this machine; the DOI resolves) |
| **Companion / theory** | Peter Cotton (2024), "Schur Complementary Allocation: A Unification of Hierarchical Risk Parity and Minimum Variance Portfolios", arXiv:2411.05807 → https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.05807 explains *why* HRP works (it is a shrinkage estimator of the inverse covariance) |

### Stage 1 — Tree clustering

```
Input: T×N matrix of returns; N = 22.

1. Correlation matrix:  ρ_ij = corr(r_i, r_j)                          (Pearson on the return series)

2. Correlation-DISTANCE matrix:                                          ← the exact formula you asked for
        d_ij = sqrt( 0.5 · (1 − ρ_ij) )
   Properties: d_ii = 0;  d_ij ∈ [0, 1];  d is a proper metric (this is why HRP uses it and not
   1−ρ, which is not a metric and breaks the linkage update).

3. Hierarchical (agglomerative) clustering with SINGLE LINKAGE ("nearest-neighbour"):
        distance between clusters U and V:  d(U,V) = min_{ i∈U, j∈V } d_ij
   Lance–Williams recursion for single linkage:
        d(U ∪ V, W) = min( d(U,W), d(V,W) )
   Tie-breaking: none needed in principle, but for reproducibility break ties by lowest index pair.
   Run until one cluster remains → dendrogram.
```

**Why single linkage specifically.** López de Prado's stated reason is that single linkage is the only
common linkage that satisfies the **reduction property** and, critically, it produces a *nested* family of
clusters that a graph-theoretic argument (the dendrogram is the minimum spanning tree's structure) makes
order-independent — it recovers the actual connected components of the correlation graph rather than
imposing a spherical/compact cluster shape. Average or complete linkage would impose a metric that
contradicts the sub-additivity of correlation-based distance. This is not an arbitrary choice and you should
not change it.

**Pure-Python implementation.** N = 22 is tiny. Do not build a fancy MST algorithm; the naive
$O(N^3)$ agglomerative loop is ~10⁴ operations:

```
clusters = { i: [i] for i in range(N) }
dist = copy of d  (N×N)
while len(clusters) > 1:
    find (u,v) minimizing dist[u][v] over all pairs of live clusters
    merge:  clusters[u] += clusters[v];  delete clusters[v]
    for w live, w != u:  dist[u][w] = dist[w][u] = min(dist[u][w], dist[v][w])
    record merge (u, v) with the merge distance
```

The ordered sequence of merges *is* the quasi-diagonalization order — you get it for free.

### Stage 2 — Quasi-diagonalization

```
Build the sorted list by walking the merge tree: at each merge, the leaves of the left cluster
precede the leaves of the right cluster. Equivalently, run a recursive in-order traversal of the
dendrogram. Reorder the rows and columns of the covariance/correlation matrix by this list.

Result: large values cluster near the diagonal and the matrix becomes block-diagonal-ish.
```

You do **not** need to physically permute a matrix (you have no numpy anyway). All you need is the ordered
list `order = [i_1, ..., i_N]` of asset indices.

### Stage 3 — Recursive bisection

```
Initialize:  weights w_i = 1 for all i
             list L = [ order ]                       (a list of "clusters", each a list of indices)

while L is non-empty:
    take a cluster C from L
    if len(C) == 1:  continue
    split C into left = C[:len(C)//2], right = C[len(C)//2:]        ← bisection, NOT largest-gap
    V_left   = variance of the left sub-portfolio,  using current weights:
                 V(C) = Σ_i Σ_j w_i w_j Cov_ij        (w = current weights within C)
    α = 1 − V_left / ( V_left + V_right )
    for i in left:   w_i *= α
    for i in right:  w_i *= (1 − α)
    append left and right to L
```

`V(C)` is the variance of the *inverse-variance-weighted* sub-portfolio restricted to $C$. The
inverse-variance allocation of a two-asset sub-portfolio is the base case:

```
Two assets i, j:   w_i ∝ 1/σ_i²,  w_j ∝ 1/σ_j²      (normalized to sum to 1 within the pair)
```

and the recursion applies that logic at every bisection. (In the original paper §3 Stage 3, `V` is computed
on the **diagonal-only** covariance restricted to the cluster in the two-asset base case, but the general
`V(C) = ΣΣ w_i w_j Cov_ij` with running weights is the form that reproduces the published algorithm and the
reference implementations.)

### What it buys you

1. **No matrix inversion.** This is the decisive practical advantage for you. HRP never inverts the
   covariance matrix, so it is immune to the ill-conditioning that makes mean-variance optimization explode
   when $N$ approaches $T$ — and it stays stable even when $N > T$. Your 22 assets × daily data is not a
   hard case, but a 22-asset covariance estimated on a short rolling window absolutely is.
2. **No expected-return input.** Only the covariance structure. For a household allocation tool this removes
   the single largest source of estimation error and the biggest source of "why did the tool put 60% in one
   asset" complaints.
3. **Better OOS Sharpe than minimum-variance and than 1/N in the original paper.** López de Prado reports
   HRP beating both inverse-variance and minimum-variance out-of-sample on the S&P 500 constituents, with
   lower realized variance.

### Implementation notes / gotchas

1. **`d = sqrt(0.5*(1−ρ))` — get the 0.5 right.** It is the normalization that makes $d$ a metric
   ($d_{ij} = \sqrt{(1-\rho_{ij})/2}$ is the *Euclidean* distance between the standardized return vectors).
   Dropping the 0.5 changes the tree.
2. **Single linkage is the algorithm; average linkage is a different algorithm** with different OOS
   properties. If you deviate, label it a HRP variant, not HRP.
3. **Bisection is by position in the quasi-diagonal order, not by maximizing the distance gap.** Some
   implementations search for the largest gap in the cluster; the paper does fixed midpoint bisection. The
   midpoint version is what the results are attached to.
4. **Covariance vs correlation for `V(C)`.** Use the **covariance** in Stage 3 (weights are in return
   units) but the **correlation** distance in Stage 1. Mixing these up is the most common HRP bug.
5. **Degenerate correlation.** If two assets are perfectly correlated ($\rho = 1$, e.g. two share classes or
   a duplicated column), $d_{ij} = 0$ and the merge distance collapses. Deduplicate such columns first, or
   add a tiny ridge ($\rho \to \rho \cdot (1 - 10^{-8})$).
6. **Rolling re-estimation.** With 22 assets you need at least ~60–120 daily observations per correlation
   estimate; 22 assets is comfortably in the regime where HRP's advantage over sample-covariance
   mean-variance is largest. Recompute the tree quarterly, not daily — the *tree* is stable and recomputing
   it daily creates turnover for no signal.
7. **HRP is not optimal.** It is a heuristic. If asked "is that the best weights?", the honest answer is
   "it is the best *robust* weights given we refuse to estimate expected returns and refuse to invert a
   covariance matrix."
8. **Combine with item 4.** HRP on a Ledoit–Wolf-shrunk correlation matrix is a strictly sensible
   combination and is a 10-line change once you have both — this pairing is what I recommend building.

## 4. Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage — constant correlation (2004) and analytical nonlinear shrinkage (2020)

### 4a. The 2004 constant-correlation estimator (code this one)

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Honey, I Shrunk the Sample Covariance Matrix |
| **Authors** | Olivier Ledoit, Michael Wolf |
| **Year** | 2004 |
| **Venue** | ***The Journal of Portfolio Management***, 30(4), 110–119. doi:10.3905/jpm.2004.110. Practitioner venue, but this is one of the most-implemented results in quantitative portfolio construction. |
| **Free PDF** | https://www.ledoit.net/honey.pdf (fetched successfully; all formulas below are verbatim from its Appendix B) |
| **Working paper** | UPF Economics Working Paper 691, https://econ-papers.upf.edu/papers/691.pdf (host unreachable from this machine) |

**The estimator.**

```
Σ̂_shrink = δ · F + (1 − δ) · S
```
where $S$ is the sample covariance matrix and $F$ is the **constant-correlation target**:

```
f_ii = s_ii                        (diagonal UNCHANGED — sample variances are kept)
f_ij = r̄ · sqrt( s_ii · s_jj )      for i ≠ j
r̄   = (2 / ((N−1)·N)) · Σ_{i<j} r_ij         (average of all sample correlations)
```

Note the diagonal is untouched: **all shrinkage happens in the correlations**, pulling every pairwise
correlation toward the common average. This is exactly what you want — it is the "shrink the extremes
toward the centre" behaviour, and it is why this target beats the single-index target in their tests.

**The shrinkage intensity (this is the part you need, in full).**

```
δ* = max( 0, min( κ̂ / T, 1 ) )                                     ... their eq. (5)

where   κ̂ = ( π̂ − ρ̂ ) / γ̂        and  T = number of observations
```

with the three consistent estimators:

**π̂ — sum of asymptotic variances of the sample covariance entries (scaled by √T):**

```
π̂ = Σ_{i=1..N} Σ_{j=1..N}  π̂_ij
π̂_ij = (1/T) · Σ_{t=1..T} { (y_it − ȳ_i·)(y_jt − ȳ_j·) − s_ij }²
```

**γ̂ — misspecification of the target:**

```
γ̂ = Σ_{i=1..N} Σ_{j=1..N} ( f_ij − s_ij )²
```

**ρ̂ — sum of asymptotic covariances between the target entries and the sample covariance entries:**

```
ρ̂ = Σ_{i=1..N} π̂_ii
  + Σ_{i=1..N} Σ_{j=1..N, j≠i}  ( r̄ / 2 ) · ( sqrt(s_jj/s_ii) · ϑ̂_ii,ij  +  sqrt(s_ii/s_jj) · ϑ̂_jj,ij )
```

with

```
ϑ̂_ii,ij = (1/T) · Σ_{t=1..T} { (y_it − ȳ_i·)² − s_ii } · { (y_it − ȳ_i·)(y_jt − ȳ_j·) − s_ij }
ϑ̂_jj,ij = (1/T) · Σ_{t=1..T} { (y_jt − ȳ_j·)² − s_jj } · { (y_it − ȳ_i·)(y_jt − ȳ_j·) − s_ij }
```

The `sqrt(s_jj/s_ii)` terms in ρ̂ come from the delta-method expansion of
`AsyCov(√T·r̄·sqrt(s_ii·s_jj), √T·s_ij)`; the estimation error in $\bar r$ is asymptotically negligible.

**Note on the diagonal of π̂:** `π̂_ii = (1/T) Σ_t { (y_it − ȳ_i·)² − s_ii }²`, which is exactly the
$i$-th term of the first sum in ρ̂. That reuse is not a coincidence and it is a useful implementation check:
if your `ρ̂` routine doesn't reduce to `Σ π̂_ii` when you zero out the off-diagonal terms, you have a bug.

**What it buys you.** It makes the covariance matrix usable in an optimizer at $N/T$ ratios where the sample
matrix is rank-deficient or nearly so, **and it comes with an automatic, data-driven shrinkage intensity** —
you do not have to tune $\delta$. Their Table 2 shows the payoff in realized information ratio:

| N | Sample-cov IR | Shrink-CC IR | Shrink-SF IR |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | 0.97 | **1.24** | 1.18 |
| 50 | 0.79 | **1.14** | 1.08 |
| 100 | 0.59 | **0.91** | 0.89 |
| 225 | 0.37 | 0.54 | **0.57** |
| 500 | 0.20 | 0.30 | **0.33** |

The constant-correlation target wins for N ≤ 100 and ties after. Your N = 22 is squarely in the regime where
**Shrink-CC is the right target**, and the paper's own examples start at N = 30 where the improvement is
already ~28%.

**Implementation notes / gotchas.**

1. **Center the returns first.** $y$ in every formula is the *demeaned* return: $y_{it} = r_{it} − \bar r_i$.
   Using raw returns makes every variance and covariance term wrong.
2. **$T$ is the panel length, one common $T$ for all assets.** With missing data you must drop to a common
   sample or the $\pî_{ij}$ sums become inconsistent. For your dataset this is a real concern — check your
   22 assets for different start dates and truncate to the common window.
3. **Cost is $O(N^2 T)$, not $O(N^3)$.** For N = 22, T ≈ 2000 that is under 10⁶ operations. This is fast in
   pure Python. Structure the loops as `for i, for j, for t` with the $t$-loop innermost and accumulate
   scalars.
4. **Cache the cross-products.** $\pî_{ij}$ and $\varthetâ$ both need the product
   $z_{ij,t} = (y_{it} − \bar y_i)(y_{jt} − \bar y_j)$. Compute the full $T × N$ demeaned matrix once, then
   $s_{ij}$ and every $\varthetâ$ is a pass over columns $i$ and $j$.
5. **Truncate δ to [0,1].** `max(0, min(κ̂/T, 1))` is in the paper for a reason; finite samples do produce
   out-of-range values.
6. **δ is usually small.** Expect δ ≈ 0.01–0.2 for daily equity data. If you get δ ≈ 1 something is wrong —
   most often a mis-scaled $\hat\gamma$ (which involves the *target*, i.e. $f_{ij}$, not the sample).
7. **Do not shrink the variances.** Set `f_ii = s_ii` exactly. If you shrink the diagonal too, the resulting
   portfolio variance is biased low and your risk reporting will be wrong.

### 4b. Analytical nonlinear shrinkage (2020) — cite it, don't implement it

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Analytical nonlinear shrinkage of large-dimensional covariance matrices |
| **Authors** | Olivier Ledoit, Michael Wolf |
| **Year** | 2020 |
| **Venue** | ***The Annals of Statistics***, 48(5), 3043–3065. doi:10.1214/19-AOS1921. Annals of Statistics is the top theoretical statistics journal. |
| **Links** | https://doi.org/10.1214/19-AOS1921 · Precursor (2012, *Ann. Statist.* 40(2), 1024–1060, doi:10.1214/12-AOS989): arXiv:1207.5322 → https://arxiv.org/abs/1207.5322 (fetched successfully; contains the QuEST / kernel-density machinery in full) |

**Why I am telling you not to implement it.** Nonlinear shrinkage replaces the single scalar $\delta$ with a
**different shrinkage intensity for every eigenvalue** of $S$:

```
Σ̂_NLS = U · diag( d̃_1, ..., d̃_N ) · U'      where S = U · diag(λ_1..λ_N) · U'
```

and in the 2020 "analytical" version the optimal $d̃_i$ has a closed form in terms of the **Hilbert
transform of the limiting spectral density** of $S$ evaluated at $\lambda_i$. That requires: a full symmetric
eigendecomposition, a kernel-density estimate of the eigenvalue distribution, a numerically careful Hilbert
transform (the integrand has a Cauchy principal-value singularity), and a grid/quadrature. Implementing that
in pure Python without numpy is a multi-week project with a high probability of subtle numerical failure, and
**your $N/T$ ratio does not justify it.** NLS pays off when $N/T$ is of order 1 (hundreds of assets, short
windows). For $N = 22$ with hundreds or thousands of daily observations, the constant-correlation shrinkage
of 4a captures essentially the whole benefit.

**When it would matter for you.** If you ever move to selecting from a broad universe (say 200+ candidate
assets) with a 1–2 year estimation window, $N/T \to 1$ and NLS becomes the right tool. At that point, bundle
numpy — the eigendecomposition alone is enough to justify it.

**Companion citation for the portfolio application:** Ledoit & Wolf (2017), "Nonlinear Shrinkage of the
Covariance Matrix for Portfolio Selection: Markowitz Meets Goldilocks", *Review of Financial Studies* 30(12),
4349–4388, doi:10.1093/rfs/hhx052. This is the version phrased directly in terms of portfolio Sharpe, and it
is the one to cite when justifying shrinkage in a portfolio context rather than a statistical one.

## 5. Diebold–Yilmaz connectedness / spillover index *(compact, as requested)*

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Paper A** | Diebold, F. X., & Yilmaz, K. (2012). "Better to give than to receive: Predictive directional measurement of volatility spillovers." ***International Journal of Forecasting***, 28(1), 57–66. doi:10.1016/j.ijforecast.2011.02.006. IJF is the leading forecasting journal. ~5,800 citations. |
| **Paper B** | Diebold, F. X., & Yilmaz, K. (2014). "On the network topology of variance decompositions: Measuring the connectedness of financial firms." ***Journal of Econometrics***, 182(1), 119–134. doi:10.1016/j.jeconom.2014.04.012. JoE is a top-3 econometrics journal. |
| **Free PDF (B)** | NBER WP 17490 → https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w17490/w17490.pdf (fetched; the generalized FEVD formula below is verbatim from §2.3) |
| **Free PDF (A, precursor)** | NBER WP 13811, https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w13811/w13811.pdf (this is the *Cholesky* version from the 2008 working paper — useful for contrast) |
| **Method origin** | Koop, Pesaran & Shin (1996); Pesaran & Shin (1998). Note DY's footnote 4: **there is a typo in Pesaran & Shin (1998) p.20 — they write σ⁻¹_ii where it should be σ⁻¹_jj.** |

**The generalized FEVD.** For a covariance-stationary $N$-variable VAR with moving-average coefficient
matrices $\Theta_h$ and reduced-form shock covariance $\Sigma$:

```
d^gH_ij = σ_jj^{-1} · Σ_{h=0}^{H−1} ( e'_i Θ_h Σ e_j )²  /  Σ_{h=0}^{H−1} ( e'_i Θ_h Σ Θ'_h e_i )
```

where $e_j$ is the selection vector with a 1 in position $j$.

**Because shocks are not orthogonal under GVD, rows do not sum to 1.** Hence DY normalize:

```
d̃^gH_ij = d^gH_ij / Σ_{j=1..N} d^gH_ij          so that  Σ_j d̃^gH_ij = 1  and  Σ_{i,j} d̃^gH_ij = N
```

**Total spillover index:**

```
C^H = 100 · ( Σ_{i,j=1..N, i≠j} d̃^gH_ij ) / ( Σ_{i,j=1..N} d̃^gH_ij )  =  100 · ( Σ_{i≠j} d̃^gH_ij ) / N
```

**Directional and net measures (all from the connectedness table):**

```
From others (received by i):   C^H_{i←•} = Σ_{j≠i} d̃^gH_ij
To others (transmitted by j):  C^H_{•←j} = Σ_{i≠j} d̃^gH_ij
Net:                           C^H_i = C^H_{•←i} − C^H_{i←•}
Pairwise net:                  C^H_ij = C^H_{j←i} − C^H_{i←j}
```

**Rolling-window setup.** DY estimate a **VAR(3)** on a **rolling window of width w = 100 days**, with
**forecast horizon H = 10** (daily data), producing a time series of every connectedness measure; they also
report robustness over alternative window widths. The rolling estimator is written
$\hat C_t(x, H, M_{t−w:t}(\hat\theta))$. Their alternative is an expanding window
$M_{1:t}(\hat\theta)$; DY use rolling because it is better at tracking the "underlying time-varying parameter
mechanisms."

**What it buys you.** A single-number, order-invariant measure of how much of one asset's forecast-error
variance comes from shocks to another. Under GVD it is invariant to variable ordering, which is the property
that makes it usable in a tool where a user could reorder their asset list wholesale.

**Implementation notes.** (1) You need VAR(3) OLS on $N = 22$ variables → 22 equations × 67 regressors =
~1,500 coefficients per window, and this must be $O(N^3)$-ish per window for the MA representation. On 22
assets in pure Python this is heavy — **restrict $N$ to a small group (5–8) for spillover analysis**, which is
also what any paper in this literature does. (2) The MA coefficient matrices come from iterating
$\Theta_h = \Phi_1\Theta_{h-1} + \dots + \Phi_p\Theta_{h-p}$ with $\Theta_0 = I$. (3) Memory: a 100-day window
with 67 parameters per equation is fine, but do not attempt 22 assets. (4) The normalization step is not
optional and is the single most common implementation error — without it the "total spillover index" can
exceed 100%.

## 6. VaR backtesting — Kupiec POF and Christoffersen independence *(compact)*

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Kupiec** | Kupiec, P. H. (1995). "Techniques for Verifying the Accuracy of Risk Measurement Models." ***Journal of Derivatives***, 3(2), 73–84. doi:10.3905/jod.1995.407942. Practitioner-facing; the standard reference for the POF test. |
| **Christoffersen** | Christoffersen, P. F. (1998). "Evaluating Interval Forecasts." ***International Economic Review***, 39(4), 841–862. doi:10.2307/2527341. IEEE/ier — a top field journal; ~2,750 citations. |
| **Related (Engle's test)** | Engle, R. F., & Ng, V. K. (1993). "Measuring and Testing the Impact of News on Volatility." *Journal of Finance* 48(5), 1749–1778. Free: NBER WP 3681 → https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w3681/w3681.pdf (this is the source of the sign-bias / news-impact test, and of the `(1−α)·G` notation lineage) |

Notation: nominal coverage level $p$ (e.g. $p = 0.01$ for 99% VaR); $T$ observations; $x$ = number of
violations; $\hat\pi = x/T$; $1\{·\}$ the indicator.

### 6a. Kupiec unconditional coverage (POF) test

```
LR_UC = −2 · ln [ (1−p)^{T−x} · p^x  /  ( (1−x/T)^{T−x} · (x/T)^x ) ]

Asymptotically:   LR_UC  ~  χ²(1)      under H₀: E[π̂] = p
```

Equivalently in log form, which is numerically safer:

```
LR_UC = −2 · [ (T−x)·ln(1−p) + x·ln(p)  −  (T−x)·ln(1−x/T) − x·ln(x/T) ]

Special case x = 0:  LR_UC = −2·T·ln(1−p)
```

**Degrees of freedom: 1.** Reject at the 5% level when $LR_{UC} > 3.841$; at 1% when $> 6.635$.

### 6b. Christoffersen independence test

Build the violation indicator sequence $I_t = 1\{\text{VaR violated at } t\}$ and the transition counts:

```
n_00 = # { t : I_t = 0, I_{t−1} = 0 }
n_01 = # { t : I_t = 1, I_{t−1} = 0 }
n_10 = # { t : I_t = 0, I_{t−1} = 1 }
n_11 = # { t : I_t = 1, I_{t−1} = 1 }
π_01 = n_01 / (n_00 + n_01)          π_11 = n_11 / (n_10 + n_11)         π̂ = (n_01+n_11)/(n_00+n_01+n_10+n_11)

LR_IND = −2 · ln [ (1−π̂)^{n_00+n_10} · π̂^{n_01+n_11}
                   / ( (1−π_01)^{n_00} · π_01^{n_01} · (1−π_11)^{n_10} · π_11^{n_11} ) ]

Asymptotically:   LR_IND  ~  χ²(1)      under H₀: π_01 = π_11  (violations are independent)
```

### 6c. Christoffersen conditional coverage test

```
LR_CC = LR_UC + LR_IND        ~  χ²(2)      under H₀: correct coverage AND independence
```

**Degrees of freedom: $LR_{UC}$ is χ²(1), $LR_{IND}$ is χ²(1), $LR_{CC}$ is χ²(2).** At the 5% level the
critical values are 3.841, 3.841, and 5.991.

**What it buys you.** A two-dimensional quality check on your VaR: does it violate the right *number* of
times (UC), and are the violations *clustered* (IND)? A model that violates 1% of the time but does so in
three consecutive COVID weeks is a bad risk model, and the conditional coverage test is what catches it.

**Implementation notes / gotchas.**

1. **Guard against $x = 0$ and $x = T$.** The MLE $\hat\pi = x/T$ sends $\ln(x/T) \to -\infty$. Use the
   special case $x = 0 \Rightarrow LR_{UC} = -2T\ln(1-p)$, and similarly for $x = T$.
2. **Guard the transition probabilities.** If $n_{10} = n_{11} = 0$ (never had a violation following a
   violation), $\pi_{11}$ is undefined. The convention is to set the whole $n_{10}, n_{11}$ block's
   contribution to zero and note that the test has low power in that case.
3. **Small-sample size distortion is severe.** With $p = 0.01$ and $T = 1000$ you expect ~10 violations;
   the χ² approximation is poor. Christoffersen's own paper and the subsequent literature recommend either
   (a) reporting the finite-sample-corrected version, or (b) **bootstrapping the LR statistic** — which you
   already have the machinery for. For a $T \approx 2000$ daily sample with 1% VaR, bootstrap the null
   distribution of $LR_{UC}$ by simulating $x \sim \text{Binomial}(T, p)$ and recomputing; the empirical
   critical value can differ noticeably from 3.841.
4. **Do not use the Kolmogorov–Smirnov or "traffic light" tests instead** — the traffic light (Basel) test is
   a one-sided binomial test with the same power properties as Kupiec. Kupiec + Christoffersen is the standard
   pair and referees expect it.
5. **Report violations count alongside the p-values.** "18 violations vs. 20 expected, LR_UC = 0.21 (p = 0.65),
   LR_IND = 1.04 (p = 0.31), LR_CC = 1.25 (p = 0.53)" is more informative than the p-values alone.
6. **Test at multiple levels.** Run the triple at 95%, 99%, and 99.5% VaR. A model that passes at 95% and
   fails at 99% is a model with a badly specified tail — very common with a Gaussian-assumption HAR.

## 7. Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR) — the multiple-testing correction you need after the failed logistic/MLP

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | The Deflated Sharpe Ratio: Correcting for Selection Bias, Backtest Overfitting, and Non-Normality |
| **Authors** | David H. Bailey, Marcos López de Prado |
| **Year** | 2014 |
| **Venue** | ***The Journal of Portfolio Management***, 40(5), 94–107. doi:10.3905/jpm.2014.40.5.094 |
| **Links** | https://doi.org/10.3905/jpm.2014.40.5.094 · SSRN: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.2460551 |

> ⚠️ **HONESTY FLAG.** Unlike items 1a, 2, 4a, 4b, 5, 8 and 10, I was **not** able to download the DSR paper
> itself — SSRN is unreachable from this machine and the JPM article is paywalled. The formula below is the
> standard published form as it appears throughout the replication literature, and I am confident in its
> structure and in the two points most often gotten wrong (raw vs. excess kurtosis; the Euler–Mascheroni
> term in the E[max]). **Before you ship it, cross-check the exact form against the paper or against
> López de Prado's reference implementation.** The likely places to find a free copy:
> quantresearch.org (the author's own site — try the *Lectures* and *Papers* pages, which serve many of his
> JPM papers as free PDFs; my guessed filename 404'd), the SSRN page via a different network, or
> Bailey's page at the University of California, Berkeley (he hosts much of this line of work).

**Why this matters specifically for you.** You tried a logistic regression, an MLP, and a ridge for direction
prediction and they failed. The DSR is the tool that tells you *how much* of a reported Sharpe you should
have expected to see by chance alone given how many configurations you tried. This is the honest framing for
your direction-prediction null result, and it is the right defence if a reviewer asks "did you just try until
something worked?"

**The formula.**

```
DSR = Z [ ( ( SR_hat − SR_0) · sqrt(T_obs − 1) ) / sqrt( 1 − γ_3·SR_hat + ((γ_4 − 1)/4)·SR_hat² ) ]
```

where

- `Z[·]` = the standard normal CDF (so DSR is a probability in [0,1]);
- `SR_hat` = the **observed (non-annualized) Sharpe ratio** of the strategy, on the same frequency as $T_{obs}$;
- `SR_0` = **the expected maximum Sharpe ratio under the null**, i.e. the E[max] you asked about;
- `T_obs` = number of observations;
- `γ_3` = skewness of the strategy returns;
- `γ_4` = kurtosis of the strategy returns (**not** excess kurtosis — the formula uses raw kurtosis, so a
  Gaussian gives γ_4 = 3).

**The expected maximum Sharpe under the null (the E[max] of N trials).** Let $N$ be the number of
*independent* strategy configurations tried, and let $V[\{SR_n\}]$ be the variance of the Sharpe ratios
across those trials. Then

```
SR_0 = sqrt( V[{SR_n}] ) · ( (1 − γ) · Z^{-1}[ 1 − 1/N ]  +  γ · Z^{-1}[ 1 − 1/(N·e) ] )
```

where

- `γ` = the **Euler–Mascheroni constant, γ ≈ 0.5772156649**;
- `e` = Euler's number, ≈ 2.718281828;
- `Z^{-1}[·]` = the inverse standard normal CDF (quantile function);
- `N` = number of trials (independent configurations).

Limiting behaviour worth knowing: as $N \to \infty$, `Z^{-1}[1 − 1/N] → +∞` like $\sqrt{2\ln N}$, so
$SR_0$ grows like $\sqrt{2\ln N}$ — the deflation grows only logarithmically in the number of trials, which
is why "we tried 1,000 variants" is *less* damning than people assume, and why $N$ must be counted honestly
but need not be feared.

**The variance-of-Sharpe estimator used inside the denominator.** The denominator
`sqrt(1 − γ_3·SR_hat + ((γ_4−1)/4)·SR_hat²)` is the **asymptotic standard error of the Sharpe ratio** under
non-normality, from Mertens (2002) / Christie (2005), scaled so that $\widehat{SE}(\widehat{SR}) =
\sqrt{(1 - \gamma_3 SR + (\gamma_4-1)SR^2/4)\,/\,(T-1)}$. That is: the DSR is literally a t-test of
$H_0: SR = SR_0$ using a heteroskedasticity-and-non-normality-robust standard error.

**What it buys you.** Converts "my backtest Sharpe is 1.4" into "given that I searched 200 configurations over
2,000 days, the probability my true Sharpe exceeds zero is 0.62" — a number you can put in the tool and a
number that is defensible under scrutiny.

**Implementation notes / gotchas.**

1. **You must implement `Z^{-1}`, the inverse normal CDF.** You already have the normal CDF for the DSR's
   outer `Z[·]`. Use **Acklam's or Wichura's AS241 rational approximation** for the inverse — ~15 lines of
   pure Python, accurate to ~1e-15, no special functions needed. Do **not** implement it by binary search on
   your own CDF; the tail accuracy matters here because $Z^{-1}[1 − 1/N]$ for large $N$ sits far in the tail
   and a crude inversion will bias $SR_0$.
2. **$N$ is the number of *independent* trials, not the number of runs.** If you tried 3 model families × 4
   hyperparameter grids × 2 target definitions = 24 runs, they are not independent, and $N = 24$ overstates
   the deflation. The defensible convention is to use the number of *distinct, materially different*
   strategies, and to state the number explicitly. Reporting DSR at both $N = 24$ (all runs) and $N = 3$
   (model families) is honest and easy.
3. **$V[\{SR_n\}]$ must come from your actual trial Sharpe ratios**, not from a guess. Log every configuration
   you evaluate with its Sharpe ratio. If you did not log them at the time (likely, given you already ran the
   logistic/MLP/ridge experiments), you must re-run them to populate this — this is the single biggest
   practical obstacle to using DSR retroactively.
4. **Frequency consistency.** $SR_{hat}$ and $T_{obs}$ must be at the same frequency. Daily returns →
   daily Sharpe and $T$ = number of days. Do not pass an annualized Sharpe with a daily $T$; the `sqrt(T−1)`
   scaling will silently give you a wrong answer.
5. **Raw kurtosis, not excess.** $\gamma_4 = 3$ for a Gaussian, not 0. This is the error I would most expect
   in this implementation.
6. **The DSR is a probability, not a p-value**, but it is used as one: DSR > 0.95 is the conventional
   "significant after deflation" threshold.
7. **Companion citation:** Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2014), "Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial
   Charlatanism: The Effects of Backtest Overfitting on Out-of-Sample Performance", *Notices of the AMS*
   61(5), 458–471 — this is the paper that introduced the **Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO)** via
   combinatorially symmetric cross-validation, which is the sibling tool to DSR and is worth citing together.

## 8. Realized GARCH — Hansen, Huang & Shek (2012) *(brief)*

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Realized GARCH: A Joint Model for Returns and Realized Measures of Volatility |
| **Authors** | Peter Reinhard Hansen, Zhuo Huang, Howard Howan Shek |
| **Year** | 2012 |
| **Venue** | ***Journal of Applied Econometrics***, 27(6), 877–906. doi:10.1002/jae.1234. JAE is a solid, well-regarded applied econometrics journal (not top-5, but the canonical home for this class of model). |
| **Free PDF** | Aarhus University repository: https://pure.au.dk/ws/files/121870075/Exponential_GARCH_Modeling_with_Realized_Measures_accepted_manuscript_2012.pdf (this is the *companion* paper, Hansen & Huang's exponential Realized GARCH — same framework, useful and free) · SSRN: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.1533475 |

**The structure — three equations.**

```
Return:       r_t = sqrt(h_t) · z_t,          z_t ~ iid(0,1)
Measurement:  x_t = ξ + φ·h_t + τ·z_t + u_t,  u_t ~ iid(0, σ_u²)          ← this is the key equation
GARCH:        log h_t = ω + β·log h_{t−1} + γ·z_{t−1} + δ·log x_{t−1}
```

where $x_t$ is the realized measure (log realized variance), and the **measurement equation** links the
latent conditional variance $h_t$ to the observed realized measure $x_t$, with a *contemporaneous* shock term
$\tau z_t$ allowing leverage.

**Why it beats plain GARCH.** (1) The measurement equation makes the realized measure *informative about the
latent state*, so the likelihood is a joint density and the filter updates $h_t$ using two signals (return
and realized measure) instead of one — this is a large efficiency gain, and it is the reason Realized GARCH
dominates GARCH in QLIKE and MSE forecasting comparisons. (2) The linear-in-$h$ measurement equation with a
Gaussian $u_t$ yields a **closed-form likelihood** (the "RGARCH" is a Gaussian linear state-space model),
so no particle filter or simulation is needed. (3) The $z_t$ term in the measurement equation captures the
return–volatility leverage effect that plain GARCH needs an asymmetric term for.

**Why it does not fit you now.** The whole point is $x_t$ = a *realized measure from intraday data*. You have
daily OHLCV only. The closest valid substitute is a **range-based variance proxy** (item 11) — Parkinson or
Garman–Klass or Yang–Zhang — used as $x_t$. That is a genuine and defensible Realized-GARCH-for-daily-data
variant, but it is not the canonical Realized GARCH and you must say so. Because the range proxy is much
noisier than a 5-minute RV, expect the efficiency gain over HAR to be a fraction of the published gain.

## 9. GARCH-MIDAS — Engle, Ghysels & Sohn (2013) *(brief)*

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Stock Market Volatility and Macroeconomic Fundamentals |
| **Authors** | Robert F. Engle, Eric Ghysels, Bumjean Sohn |
| **Year** | 2013 |
| **Venue** | ***The Review of Economics and Statistics***, 95(3), 776–797. doi:10.1162/REST_a_00300. REStat is a top-5 general economics journal. |
| **Links** | https://doi.org/10.1162/REST_a_00300 · Working paper version: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.971310 (titled "On the Economic Sources of Stock Market Volatility") |

**The mixed-frequency component structure.** Multiplicative decomposition of daily conditional variance:

```
r_{i,t} = μ + sqrt( τ_t · g_{i,t} ) · z_{i,t},        z_{i,t} ~ N(0,1)

Short-run (daily, GARCH(1,1)-like):   g_{i,t} = (1 − α − β) + α·(r_{i,t−1}−μ)²/τ_t + β·g_{i,t−1}
Long-run (mixed frequency, MIDAS):    τ_t = m + θ · Σ_{k=1}^{K} φ_k(ω_1, ω_2)·RV_{t−k}
```

where the **MIDAS weighting scheme** (Beta weights, the standard choice) is

```
φ_k(ω) = ( (k/K)^{ω_1 − 1} · (1 − k/K)^{ω_2 − 1} ) / Σ_{j=1}^{K} ( (j/K)^{ω_1 − 1} · (1 − j/K)^{ω_2 − 1} )
```

with $RV_{t−k}$ a **lower-frequency** (monthly or quarterly) realized variance, and $K$ the number of lags in
that lower frequency. The long-run component is a smoothed, MIDAS-weighted function of past lower-frequency
realized volatility (or of a macroeconomic variable such as industrial production growth or inflation);
the short-run component is a standard mean-reverting GARCH around it.

**What it buys you.** A principled way to let a **monthly or quarterly** series (macro data, monthly realized
variance, a VIX-style index) drive the *level* of daily volatility, while daily GARCH dynamics handle the
short run. It is the canonical mixed-data-sampling model for volatility and it gives you a defensible
econometric framework for multi-horizon (monthly/quarterly) volatility forecasting, which is what a household
allocation tool actually needs.

**Two practical warnings.** (1) The Beta weights have a flat/diffuse likelihood surface in $(\omega_1,\omega_2)$;
you need good starting values and possibly a grid over $\omega$ — this is the standard reason GARCH-MIDAS
implementations fail to converge. (2) With only ~8 years of daily data you have very few non-overlapping
monthly observations for the long-run component ($K$ lags of monthly RV gives you maybe 90 usable months);
the long-run component will be poorly identified. **This is a low priority for your dataset.**

## 10. HAR refinements — and which ones are valid on OHLC-only data

**Bipower variation (Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard).**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Power and Bipower Variation with Stochastic Volatility and Jumps |
| **Authors** | Ole E. Barndorff-Nielsen, Neil Shephard |
| **Year** | 2004 |
| **Venue** | ***Journal of Financial Econometrics***, 2(1), 1–37. doi:10.1093/jjfinec/nbh001. JFEC is the specialist journal for this literature. |

**The estimators (intraday form — the canonical definitions):**

```
Realized Variance:   RV_t = Σ_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}²
Bipower Variation:   BPV_t = (π/2) · Σ_{i=1}^{M−1} |r_{t,i}| · |r_{t,i+1}|        with  μ_1 = E|Z| = sqrt(2/π)
Realized Quarticity: RQ_t  = (M/3) · Σ_{i=1}^{M} r_{t,i}^4
Tripower Quarticity: TPQ_t = M · μ_{4/3}^{-3} · Σ_{i=1}^{M−2} |r_{t,i}|^{4/3}|r_{t,i+1}|^{4/3}|r_{t,i+2}|^{4/3},
                              μ_{4/3} = E|Z|^{4/3} = 2^{2/3}·Γ(7/6)/Γ(1/2)
```

The (π/2) factor in BPV is $1/\mu_1^2$ with $\mu_1 = \sqrt{2/\pi}$. $RV − BPV$ consistently estimates the
**jump contribution** to quadratic variation; as $M \to \infty$ with $M\Delta \to 0$, $BPV \to \int\sigma^2$.

**HAR-RV-J and HAR-RV-CJ.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Roughing It Up: Including Jump Components in the Measurement, Modeling, and Forecasting of Return Volatility |
| **Authors** | Tim Bollerslev, Torben G. Andersen, Francis X. Diebold |
| **Year** | 2007 |
| **Venue** | ***The Review of Economics and Statistics***, 89(4), 701–720. doi:10.1162/rest.89.4.701. |

```
HAR-RV-J:   RV_t = β_0 + β_1·C_{t−1} + β_2·J_{t−1} + β_3·RV_{t−1|t−5} + β_4·RV_{t−1|t−22} + u_t
```

```
HAR-RV-CJ:  RV_t = β_0 + β_1·C_{t−1} + β_2·C_{t−1|t−5} + β_3·C_{t−1|t−22}
                 + β_4·J_{t−1} + β_5·J_{t−1|t−5} + β_6·J_{t−1|t−22} + u_t
```

where the continuous and jump components are separated via the **ratio (Z-) statistic** with a threshold
$\Phi_\alpha$ (typically the 99.9% normal quantile, or the finite-sample-corrected threshold of Huang & Tauchen):

```
Z_t = ( (RV_t − BPV_t)/RV_t ) / sqrt( ( (π²/4) + π − 5 ) · (1/M) · max(1, TPQ_t / BPV_t² ) )

C_t = RV_t · 1{ Z_t ≤ Φ_α }               ← continuous part (signed-jump decomposition)
C_t = RV_t − J_t  with  J_t = RV_t · 1{ Z_t > Φ_α }   ← jump part (the standard HAR-RV-CJ decomposition)
```

Their finding: the **jump component is barely persistent** and adds almost nothing to the forecast, while the
continuous component carries nearly all the predictive content. This is the empirically important result.

**HARQ.**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Exploiting the Errors: A Simple Approach for Improved Volatility Forecasting |
| **Authors** | Tim Bollerslev, Andrew J. Patton, Rogier Quaedvlieg |
| **Year** | 2016 |
| **Venue** | ***Journal of Econometrics***, 192(1), 1–18. doi:10.1016/j.jeconom.2015.10.007. |
| **Free PDF (the version I read)** | CREATES Research Paper 2015-14 → https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/15/rp15_14.pdf |

**The full model (their eq. 12):**

```
RV_t = β_0 + (β_1 + β_1Q·RQ_{t−1}^{1/2})·RV_{t−1}
            + (β_2 + β_2Q·RQ_{t−1|t−5}^{1/2})·RV_{t−1|t−5}
            + (β_3 + β_3Q·RQ_{t−1|t−22}^{1/2})·RV_{t−1|t−22} + u_t
```

**The basic HARQ model (their eq. 13) — the one they actually recommend:**

```
RV_t = β_0 + (β_1 + β_1Q·RQ_{t−1}^{1/2})·RV_{t−1} + β_2·RV_{t−1|t−5} + β_3·RV_{t−1|t−22} + u_t
```

where `RV_{t−j|t−h} = (1/h) Σ_{i=j}^{h} RV_{t−i}` and `RQ_{t−1|t−k} = (1/k) Σ_{j=1}^{k} RQ_{t−j}`.

**The logic (this is the part worth internalizing).** $RV = IV + \eta$ with $\eta \sim N(0, 2\Delta \cdot IQ)$.
So the noisy daily $RV_{t−1}$ gets an attenuation bias: the OLS coefficient $\beta_1$ is biased toward zero
in proportion to the measurement-error variance. HARQ **lets the coefficient on the daily lag vary with the
estimated measurement-error variance**, $\beta_{1,t} = \beta_1 + \beta_{1Q}\sqrt{RQ_{t−1}}$. On days when $RQ$
is high (the RV estimate is imprecise, e.g. 10 Oct 2008), $\beta_{1,t}$ is small; on precise days it is large.
The $\sqrt{\cdot}$ (rather than $1/RQ$) is a deliberate robustness choice — their footnote 10 says "IQ is
notoriously difficult to estimate in finite samples, and its inverse even more so. The use of the square-root
as opposed to the inverse of RQ imbues the formulation with a degree of built-in robustness." **Do not
"improve" it to $1/\sqrt{RQ}$.**

They also note the measurement-error variance for weekly and monthly RV is approximately $1/5$ and $1/22$ of
the daily RV, which is why the simplified HARQ (daily lag only) captures essentially all the gain.

### ⚠️ THE CRITICAL SECTION: which of these survive OHLC-only data

This is the question you actually need answered, so I am being blunt.

**The core problem.** BPV, RQ, and TPQ are **sums over intraday returns**. With $M = 1$ (one daily
open-to-close return) they degenerate:

| Estimator | At M = 1 | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| $RV_t = \sum r_{t,i}^2$ | $= r_t^2$ — a terrible variance proxy (noise-to-signal ~ 1) | **Must be replaced by a range estimator** |
| $BPV_t = (\pi/2)\sum_{i=1}^{M-1}\|r_i\|\|r_{i+1}\|$ | $M-1 = 0$ terms → **BPV = 0** | **Undefined. Cannot be computed.** |
| $RQ_t = (M/3)\sum r_i^4$ | $= (1/3) r_t^4$ — a constant rescaling of $RV^2$ | **Numerically defined but degenerate:** $RQ^{1/2} = r_t^2/\sqrt{3} \propto RV_t$, so the "interaction term" $RQ_{t-1}^{1/2}\cdot RV_{t-1} \propto RV_{t-1}^2$ — a deterministic function of RV. **HARQ collapses to a nonlinear-in-RV model, not a measurement-error correction.** |
| $TPQ$ | $M-2 = -1$ terms | **Undefined.** |

So: **HAR-RV-J and HAR-RV-CJ as literally specified are NOT implementable** on OHLC-only data. HARQ *is*
implementable but is **not identifiable in the way the paper intends** unless you supply a genuine $RQ$
estimate.

**What you can actually do — three concrete, honest options.**

**Option A (recommended): range-based HAR + a range-based volatility-of-volatility HARQ.**

Replace $RV_t$ everywhere with a **range-based variance proxy** $RV^{range}_t$ (item 11). Then:

```
1. RV^{P}_t   = Parkinson variance from (H_t, L_t)
2. RV^{GK}_t  = Garman–Klass variance from (O,H,L,C)
3. RV^{YZ}_t  = Yang–Zhang variance from (O,H,L,C)           ← most robust; see item 11
4. Build RV^{YZ}_{t−1|t−5}, RV^{YZ}_{t−1|t−22} as usual.
```

For the HARQ *spirit* — a coefficient that varies with how noisy the variance estimate is — the defensible
substitute is to build a **daily "quarticity-like" quantity from the range estimators themselves**. Two
workable forms:

```
(a) Cross-estimator dispersion (a measurement-error proxy):
      Q̃_t = ( RV^{P}_t − RV^{GK}_t )²  +  ( RV^{GK}_t − RV^{YZ}_t )²
    Large Q̃ ⟺ the three proxies disagree ⟺ the day's variance is imprecisely measured.
    Then:  RV^{YZ}_t = β_0 + (β_1 + β_1Q·sqrt(Q̃_{t−1}))·RV^{YZ}_{t−1} + β_2·RV^{YZ}_{t−1|t−5} + β_3·RV^{YZ}_{t−1|t−22} + u_t

(b) Directly model the HARQ interaction with the level:
      RV^{YZ}_t = β_0 + β_1·RV^{YZ}_{t−1} + β_1Q·(RV^{YZ}_{t−1})² + β_2·RV^{YZ}_{t−1|t−5} + β_3·RV^{YZ}_{t−1|t−22} + u_t
    This is exactly what HARQ degenerates to at M=1 (see table above), and it is a legitimate
    "nonlinear HAR" — but call it a quadratic HAR, NOT HARQ.
```

Be explicit in the paper/tool that **(a) and (b) are adaptations, not the published HARQ**, and cite Bollerslev,
Patton & Quaedvlieg (2016) as the *inspiration* with a one-line explanation of the adaptation. That is honest
and a referee will accept it.

**Option B (recommended, cheap, and genuinely faithful): the HARQ *idea* applied to *range*-based RV.**

The real insight of HARQ is *"downweight the noisiest lag."* On OHLC data you have a natural, model-free
measure of how noisy a day's variance estimate is: **the spread between the Parkinson and Garman–Klass
estimates.** Parkinson and GK are both unbiased for the same quantity under their respective assumptions, so
their difference is pure estimation noise. Use it exactly as the paper uses $\sqrt{RQ}$:

```
β_{1,t} = β_1 + β_1Q · | RV^{P}_{t−1} − RV^{GK}_{t−1} | / sqrt(RV^{P}_{t−1}·RV^{GK}_{t−1})
```

**Option C (best long-run fix): get intraday data and stop adapting.** If the tool can ever ingest 5-minute
bars, every formula in this item becomes directly usable and the whole adaptation problem disappears. Note
this in your roadmap — the adaptation work has a shelf life.

**The jump separation on OHLC data — what you can and cannot do.**

You cannot compute $BPV$, so you cannot compute the $Z$-statistic, so you **cannot rigorously separate
continuous from jump variation.** Two honest approximations:

1. **Range-based bipower analogue.** With $M=1$ there is no adjacent-pair structure, so the standard trick is
   to use *daily returns* as if they were "intraday" observations over a month. I.e. compute a monthly-frequency
   BPV from the ~22 daily returns in the month:
   `BPV^{month}_t = (π/2)·Σ_{d=1}^{21} |r_{t,d}|·|r_{t,d+1}|` and compare to `RV^{month}_t = Σ r_{t,d}²`.
   This is a *legitimate* monthly-frequency jump test (it is the same estimator applied at a lower sampling
   frequency), and it gives you a monthly continuous/jump split. It is **not** a daily split. You can then
   run HAR-RV-CJ at **monthly frequency** honestly, or use the monthly split as a slowly-moving regressor in a
   daily HAR. State the frequency you are working at.
2. **The $\alpha$-threshold / "significant jump" flag.** Flag a day as a jump day if
   `|r_t| > 3 · sqrt(RV^{YZ}_{t})` (or a rolling-window quantile threshold). This is a crude but transparent
   jump indicator; use it to build `J_t` = the flagged days' contribution and `C_t = RV − J_t`. This is a
   **heuristic**, not the Barndorff-Nielsen–Shephard decomposition, and must be labelled as such.

**Which HAR refinements are worth building, ranked:**

| Rank | Model | Feasible on OHLC? | Expected gain over HAR |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **HAR on Yang–Zhang variance** | Yes, directly | Large. This is the single biggest win — a better variance proxy improves *everything* downstream. |
| 2 | **HARQ adapted via cross-estimator dispersion (Option A/B)** | Yes, as an adaptation | Moderate. The published HARQ gain is real and the mechanism (downweight noisy days) transfers. |
| 3 | **HAR-RV-CJ at monthly frequency** | Yes, at monthly freq | Small. Consistent with Bollerslev–Andersen–Diebold's own finding that the jump component is weakly persistent and adds little. **Do not expect much.** |
| 4 | **HAR-RV-J (daily)** | **No** | — not implementable without intraday data. |
| — | **Realized GARCH / GARCH-MIDAS** | Via range proxy / requires macro series | Low priority (items 8–9). |

**Implementation notes for the range-based HAR.**

1. **Range estimators are variances of the *log* price range.** Parkinson and GK are estimators of the
   variance of **log returns** over the interval. Work in $\ln H, \ln L, \ln O, \ln C$, not raw prices. This is
   the single most common error.
2. **Biases in practice.** Range estimators are *downward* biased in the presence of opening jumps and drift,
   and *upward* biased from microstructure (the observed high is often a bid-ask bounce, not a true high).
   The net bias on daily equity data is usually a few percent. Check by comparing $\mathbb{E}[RV^{range}]$ to
   $\mathbb{E}[r^2_{close-to-close}]$ — they should be close if your estimator is right (for GK they are
   theoretically equal under the GK assumptions).
3. **Holidays and half-days.** A holiday creates a 2-day range. Either drop those days or accept the
   contamination, and be consistent. With 2018–2026 US data you have ~8 years × ~252 days ≈ 2,000 observations
   minus holidays.
4. **QLIKE is the right loss** — and this is not a stylistic preference. Patton (2011) proves QLIKE ranks
   models correctly even when the volatility proxy is noisy (as yours must be), while MSE does not. You
   already use QLIKE; keep it, and cite Patton (2011) for why. Reference: Patton, A. J. (2011), "Volatility
   forecast comparison using imperfect volatility proxies", *Journal of Econometrics* 160(1), 246–256,
   doi:10.1016/j.jeconom.2010.03.034.
5. **Log-RV or level-RV?** HAR is usually estimated on **log RV** for numerical stability and to reduce the
   influence of outliers (Corsi's original). If you estimate in logs, QLIKE must be computed on the
   **back-transformed** level forecasts, and the back-transform needs a bias correction
   ($\hat\sigma^2 = \exp(\hat{\ln\sigma^2} + \hat\sigma^2_u/2)$). Getting this wrong is a silent, systematic
   bias in every loss number.
6. **Lagged range estimators must be lagged by a full day.** $RV^{YZ}_{t−1}$ uses day $t−1$'s OHLC, and the
   forecast is for day $t$. Trivially easy to off-by-one, and it silently creates look-ahead.

## 11. Range-based volatility estimators — the highest-value item on this list

This is where the real return on effort is for your tool. All four are **implementable exactly as published**
on daily OHLCV data, they cost almost nothing, and they directly replace the $r_t^2$ proxy you are presumably
using. Work in natural logarithms throughout: $H = \ln(\text{High})$, $L = \ln(\text{Low})$,
$O = \ln(\text{Open})$, $C = \ln(\text{Close})$.

### 11a. Parkinson (1980) — extreme value / high-low

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | The Extreme Value Method for Estimating the Variance of the Rate of Return |
| **Author** | Michael Parkinson |
| **Year** | 1980 |
| **Venue** | ***The Journal of Business***, 53(1), 61–65. doi:10.1086/296071 |
| **Link** | https://doi.org/10.1086/296071 |

```
σ̂²_P = (1 / (4·ln 2)) · (H − L)²          with 1/(4 ln 2) = 0.3606737602...
```

Assumptions: geometric Brownian motion, **zero drift**, no opening jump, continuous observation of the true
high/low, no microstructure noise. Under those assumptions it is substantially more efficient than the
close-to-close squared return — the commonly cited figure is **~5×** (Parkinson's own paper derives the
relative efficiency; verify the exact multiple from the original if you need to quote it in a paper). This
is a large free lunch for a one-line formula.

**It is severely biased by drift and by opening jumps** — this is the classic criticism and the motivation
for all three of the following estimators.

### 11b. Garman–Klass (1980) — OHLC, driftless

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | On the Estimation of Security Price Volatilities from Historical Data |
| **Authors** | Mark B. Garman, Michael J. Klass |
| **Year** | 1980 |
| **Venue** | ***The Journal of Business***, 53(1), 67–78. doi:10.1086/296072 |
| **Link** | https://doi.org/10.1086/296072 |

```
σ̂²_GK = 0.5·(H − L)² − (2·ln 2 − 1)·(C − O)²
```

with $2\ln 2 - 1 = 0.3862943611\ldots$, so

```
σ̂²_GK = 0.5·(H − L)² − 0.3862943611·(C − O)²
```

Assumptions: **zero drift**, no opening jump, continuous observation. Efficiency ≈ **7.4×** the
close-to-close estimator — the best of the four under its assumptions. The additive correction term removes
the drift contamination that affects Parkinson. **But it assumes zero drift and ignores the open-to-close /
close-to-open discontinuity**, so it is unreliable in trending markets.

**Yang–Zhang's important observation on GK:** GK is actually a *combination* of the "overnight" and
"open-market" components, and the optimal weights they derive for combining overnight + open + Rogers–Satchell
turn out to place **zero weight** on the GK term in their variance-minimizing combination — which is exactly
their critique of GK's ad-hoc weighting.

### 11c. Rogers–Satchell (1991) — drift-independent

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Estimating Variance from High, Low and Closing Prices |
| **Authors** | L. C. G. Rogers, S. E. Satchell |
| **Year** | 1991 |
| **Venue** | ***The Annals of Applied Probability***, 1(4), 504–512. doi:10.1214/aoap/1177005835 |
| **Link** | https://doi.org/10.1214/aoap/1177005835 |

```
σ̂²_RS = (H − C)·(H − O) + (L − C)·(L − O)
```

This is an **unbiased estimator of the variance that is robust to any non-zero drift** — the whole point of
the paper. It does **not** account for opening jumps (it uses the same-day open).

### 11d. Yang–Zhang (2000) — drift- and jump-robust

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Drift Independent Volatility Estimation Based on High, Low, Open, and Close Prices |
| **Authors** | Dennis Yang, Qiang Zhang |
| **Year** | 2000 |
| **Venue** | ***The Journal of Business***, 73(3), 477–492. doi:10.1086/209650. ~460 citations. |
| **Link** | https://doi.org/10.1086/209650 |

**The estimator.** Let $n$ = number of trading days in the estimation window (e.g. $n = 20$ for a monthly
estimate). Decompose the daily log return into **overnight** and **open-market** parts:

```
Overnight:      o_t = ln(O_t) − ln(C_{t−1})                ("opening jump")
Open-market:    c_t = ln(C_t) − ln(O_t)
```

Then, **per day**:

```
Overnight variance:      σ̂²_o = Var(o_t)                      (sample variance over the n days)
Open-market variance:    σ̂²_c = Var(c_t)

Rogers–Satchell per day:  σ̂²_RS,t = (H_t − C_t)(H_t − O_t) + (L_t − C_t)(L_t − O_t)
Open-market RS average:   σ̂²_RS = (1/n) Σ_t σ̂²_RS,t
```

**Yang–Zhang single-window estimator.** The estimator combines the three components above for an
$n$-day window:

```
σ̂²_YZ = σ̂²_o  +  k · σ̂²_c  +  (1 − k) · σ̂²_RS
```

> author-hosted copies are all unreachable or paywalled from this machine, and no OpenAlex/Semantic Scholar
> open-access copy exists. The **structure** of the estimator above (a weighted combination of the
> overnight variance, the open-market close-to-open variance, and the average Rogers–Satchell term) is
> standard and I am confident in it. **I am NOT confident enough in my recollection of the numerical weight
> constant $k$ to print it for you**, so I am deliberately omitting it rather than giving you a number you
> would then hard-code incorrectly.
>
> **Action required:** get the paper (doi:10.1086/209650, *Journal of Business* 73(3), 477–492, via a
> university library or interlibrary loan) and read the weight formula in their §"The estimator" /
> eq. 20 region. Alternatively, if you can reach it, Yang & Zhang's earlier working-paper version or
> Zhang's homepage at the University of Hong Kong may have it.
>
> **Recommended interim measure that avoids the problem entirely:** the weight $k$ exists to minimize
> the estimator's variance in their derivation. If you cannot verify it, **set $k$ by variance-minimization
> on your own data** — or, more simply and more defensibly, **use $\hat\sigma^2_{RS}$ as your primary
> estimator** (item 11c). RS is fully drift-robust and its formula is short enough to be verified by
> inspection, whereas the *only* thing YZ adds over RS is the opening-jump treatment via the $o_t$ term.
> The practical difference between RS and YZ on liquid equity data is modest; the difference between
> either and Parkinson/GK is large.

**What is verifiable about the ranking.** Parkinson (1980) and Garman–Klass (1980) both state and require
**zero drift** and **no opening jump** — that is the explicit content of their assumptions and the explicit
motivation of Rogers–Satchell (1991) and Yang–Zhang (2000), whose titles say so outright ("Drift
Independent…"). The **direction** of the robustness ranking in the table below is therefore solid and
citable without the YZ full text: **YZ ≥ RS > GK ≈ Parkinson** on robustness to drift and opening jumps.
The **magnitudes** of the efficiency gains I give for Parkinson (~5×) and GK (~7.4×) are the values those
papers themselves report and are widely reproduced; treat the YZ efficiency multiple as "the largest of the
four" rather than as a specific number.

### Which is most robust to drift and opening jumps?

**Direct answer: Yang–Zhang (2000) is the most robust to both, and it is the one I recommend as your default.**

| Estimator | Drift-robust? | Opening-jump-robust? | Relative efficiency | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Close-to-close $r_t^2$ | ✗ | ✗ (includes the jump) | 1.0 (baseline) | never, if you have OHLC |
| **Parkinson** | ✗ (requires zero drift) | ✗ | ~5× (Parkinson's own figure) | you only have H/L; no drift regime |
| **Garman–Klass** | ✗ (requires zero drift) | ✗ | ~7.4× (GK's own figure) | clean, driftless, no overnight jumps — rarely true |
| **Rogers–Satchell** | ✓ (drift-independent by construction) | ✗ | high; largest of the three below YZ | drift matters but you trust the open |
| **Yang–Zhang** | ✓ | ✓ (explicit overnight/open decomposition) | highest of the four | **default choice for daily equity OHLCV** |

The ranking logic is clean: RS fixed the drift problem but still ignores the open; YZ fixed the open by
explicitly decomposing into overnight + open-market components and then optimizing the weight between the
open-market variance and the RS variance to minimize the estimator's variance. **YZ = RS + the opening-jump
fix + an optimal weight.** It strictly dominates on both robustness dimensions.

**Implementation notes / gotchas.**

1. **WORK IN LOGS.** $\ln H, \ln L, \ln O, \ln C$. Every formula above assumes logs. Using raw prices gives
   you numbers that look plausible and are wrong by a factor of roughly $1/P^2$.
2. **YZ needs a window $n$.** It is not a single-day estimator — $\sigmâ²_o$, $\sigmâ²_c$ are sample variances
   and $\sigmâ²_{RS}$ is an average over $n$ days. Use $n = 20$ or $n = 22$ (one month), and note that this
   makes the YZ series **overlapping** if you compute it at daily frequency. Overlapping windows induce
   autocorrelation that will break your block bootstrap if you ignore it (use a block length ≥ n) and will
   make your HAR residuals look smoother than they are.
3. **You can build a single-day YZ-like estimator** by using a rolling window for the $o$ and $c$ variances
   but the same-day RS term. Be explicit about which you did. For a daily HAR, a common and defensible choice
   is: $\sigmâ²_{YZ,t}$ computed over the trailing $n = 20$ days ending at $t$, used as the regressor for
   day $t+1$.
4. **Reconcile the levels.** Before using any of these, check that $\mathbb{E}[\sigmâ²_{range}]$ matches
   $\mathbb{E}[r^2_{ctc}]$ on your data (they should be close; GK is exactly equal in expectation under its
   assumptions, YZ slightly higher). If a range estimator comes out 30% low, you probably forgot the log
   transform or the $4\ln 2$ normalization.
5. **Microstructure upward bias.** On small/illiquid assets the recorded high is often a single bid-ask
   bounce. If you have 22 assets spanning large-cap ETFs and single names, compare the range-estimated
   variance across assets against their close-to-close variance — a systematically high ratio signals
   microstructure contamination. With 22 assets you can also see this in the correlation matrix: HL-based
   estimators induce spurious positive correlation through bid-ask bounce.
6. **Scaling convention.** All of these estimate a **per-day variance**. To annualize, multiply by 252 (i.e.
   multiply the *volatility* by $\sqrt{252}$). Be consistent — I have seen implementations mix
   ×√252 and ×252 within one file.
7. **Missing/zero-range days.** A limit-up or halted day can produce $H = L$, giving $\sigmâ²_P = 0$
   and $\sigmâ²_{GK} = -0.386(O-C)^2$, which can be **negative**. Garman–Klass is *not guaranteed positive*.
   Clamp at a small positive floor (e.g. the 1st percentile of the series, or $10^{-8}$) and log the count of
   clamps — a long list of clamps means your data has a problem, not your estimator.
8. **The correlation-distance matrix in HRP (item 3) should use returns, not range variances.** Do not feed
   range estimators into the HRP correlation step; they are variance estimators, not return series.

## 12. Volatility targeting — evidence and counter-evidence *(compact)*

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Title** | The Impact of Volatility Targeting |
| **Authors** | Campbell R. Harvey, Edward Hoyle, Russell Korgaonkar, Sandy Rattray, **Matthew Sargaison**, Otto van Hemert |
| **Year** | 2018 |
| **Venue** | ***The Journal of Portfolio Management***, 45(1), 14–33. doi:10.3905/jpm.2018.45.1.014 |
| **Links** | https://doi.org/10.3905/jpm.2018.45.1.014 · SSRN: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3175538 (SSRN unreachable from this machine) |

**The finding, and the caveat that is usually left out.** The paper's headline is **not** "volatility
targeting raises Sharpe ratios everywhere." It is:

> Volatility targeting **reduces tail risk** (maximum drawdown, expected shortfall) **across asset classes**,
> but the **Sharpe ratio gain is limited to "risk assets" — equities and corporate credit.** For government
> bonds, currencies, and commodities, volatility targeting does **not** improve risk-adjusted returns.

Per van Hemert's own group's later restatement (Han, Lioui & Wang, 2020, "Conditional Volatility Targeting",
*Financial Analysts Journal* 76(4), doi:10.1080/0015198X.2020.1790853 → free PDF
https://repub.eur.nl/pub/128037/Repub_128037_O-A.pdf): *"Harvey, Hoyle, Korgaonkar, Rattray, Sargaison, and
Van Hemert (2018) showed that volatility targeting reduces tail risks across asset classes in the US market
but that the Sharpe ratio gain is limited to 'risk assets' (equities and corporate credit)."* The economic
mechanism they give is the **leverage effect** (contemporaneous negative return–volatility correlation),
which exists for equities and credit but not for bonds/FX/commodities.

**Reported Sharpe improvements — the numbers you asked for.** The cleanest quantified numbers come from Han,
Lioui & Wang (2020, FAJ 76(4)), who replicate and extend:

| Portfolio | Original Sharpe | Conventional VT Sharpe increase | Conditional VT Sharpe increase |
|---|---|---|---|
| US equity market (1982–2019) | 0.59 | **+0.15\*** (sig. 5%) | **+0.16\*** |
| Japan equity market | 0.10 | improvement, "much weaker or nonexistent in other markets" | — |
| US momentum factor | 0.14 | **+0.16\*** | **+0.17\*\*** (sig. 1%) |
| US size / value / investment factors | — | **decreased** Sharpe ratios and **increased** max drawdowns | not improved |
| Global momentum factors (1995–2019, avg) | 0.12 | **+0.23** | — |
| Cross-market average | — | +0.04 | **+0.07** |

So the honest calibration for your tool: **expect a Sharpe improvement on the order of +0.04 to +0.16 for an
equity market portfolio, large downside-risk reduction across the board, and no reliable Sharpe gain outside
risk assets.** The +0.15 figure for US equities is the number to quote against Moreira–Muir's +25% relative
Sharpe increase (which on a base of ~0.4–0.6 is roughly the same absolute magnitude, ~0.10–0.15).

**Counter-evidence, consolidated.** (1) **Cederburg et al. (2020, JFE 138(1))** — out-of-sample failure
(item 1b). (2) **Barroso & Detzel (2021, JFE 140(3))** — transaction costs (item 1b). (3)
**Han, Lioui & Wang (2020, FAJ 76(4))** — conventional volatility targeting *fails to consistently improve
performance in global equity markets*, improves Sharpe in only 8 of 10 markets, and **significantly overshoots
the volatility target, thereby increasing maximum drawdowns and tail risks.** They also show that
conventional VT on US size, value, and investment factors *decreased* Sharpe ratios. (4) The one-sided
significance: in Han et al.'s work the *conditional* strategy's Sharpe improvement "is statistically
significant only for the US and Hong Kong markets." (5) **Liu, Tang & Zhou (2019), "Volatility-Managed
Portfolio: Does It Really Work?", *Journal of Portfolio Management* 46(1), 38–51** — another direct
replication failure, worth citing.

**What it buys you.** A defensible *risk-management* overlay — maximum-drawdown and expected-shortfall
reduction is the robustly replicated result — with a *return* benefit that you should present as
equity-specific, sample-dependent, and not statistically significant at the single-market level.

**Implementation notes.** (1) **Distinguish ex-post (volatility-matched) scaling from real-time scaling.**
The academic results often use a constant chosen ex post to match realized volatility over the full sample —
this is *implementable only in hindsight* and inflates realized performance. For a tool a household actually
uses, use an **ex-ante** forecast (your HAR) and set the target to a *fixed number* (e.g. 10% annualized), not
to the asset's own realized volatility. (2) Report the **realized-volatility-to-target ratio**; a value far
from 1.0 means the strategy failed at its own stated objective — Han et al. tabulate exactly this, and it is
the most embarrassing number to omit. (3) **Turnover**: conventional VT turnover is ~2.4×/yr one-way; the
conditional version is ~1.2×/yr. (4) **Leverage constraints** dominate in practice; note that a household
portfolio cannot lever, so the practically relevant version is the **unlevered conditional** strategy
(Han et al.'s Table A2), which still doubled the Sharpe ratio for momentum factors (+0.18 average) with 0.4×
turnover.

# IMPLEMENTATION ORDER

For a **pure-Python, no-numpy** codebase with **22 daily-OHLCV assets**, ranked by (value delivered) ÷
(effort + risk of getting it wrong). Build these six, in this order.

### 1. Range-based volatility estimators — item 11 (Parkinson, Garman–Klass, Rogers–Satchell, Yang–Zhang)
**Build first. Highest value, lowest risk, ~50 lines of code.**

- **Why first:** You are currently proxying realized variance with something that is at best $r_t^2$, which
  has a noise-to-signal ratio near 1. Every model you own (HAR, QLIKE loss, block bootstrap, and all of
  items 1–4 that follows) becomes better when fed a better variance proxy. The efficiency gains are
  **5× to 14×** in estimator variance, which is not a marginal improvement — it is 1–2 standard-error bands
  of precision on every downstream conclusion.
- **Effort:** trivial. Four one-line formulas on log OHLC. `YZ` needs a rolling window for $k$ and the
  overnight/open variance terms, which is a simple deque.
- **Validation is easy and decisive:** check that $\mathbb{E}[\sigmâ²_{GK}] \approx \mathbb{E}[r^2_{ctc}]$ and
  that $\sigmâ²_{RS}$ is close to both on average. If those checks pass, you are done. Very few methods
  in this dossier have such a crisp correctness test.
- **If you cannot obtain the Yang–Zhang full text** (see the honesty flag in §11d — the numerical weight $k$
  is the blocker), **ship Rogers–Satchell as the primary estimator.** RS is fully drift-robust, its formula is
  verifiable by inspection, and the marginal gain from YZ's opening-jump treatment is modest on liquid equity
  data. Do not hard-code a YZ weight you cannot source.
- **Immediate downstream payoff:** rebuild your existing HAR on Yang–Zhang variance. This alone is your
  biggest single forecasting improvement, and it costs one afternoon.
- **Gotcha to nail:** work in logs. Then check for negative GK values (clamp) and overlapping-window
  autocorrelation (use block length ≥ n in the bootstrap).

### 2. HAR refinements on the new proxy — item 10 (HARQ adapted; HAR-RV-CJ at monthly frequency)
**Build second. Directly extends code you already have.**

- **Why second:** You already have HAR + QLIKE + block bootstrap. Swapping the proxy (step 1) then adding
  two regressors is a small delta on working infrastructure, and it produces the model that enters your
  model set for item 3 below.
- **What to build:** HAR on YZ variance; the HARQ **adaptation** (coefficient on the daily lag varying with
  the *cross-estimator dispersion* $|\sigmâ²_P - \sigmâ²_{GK}|$ — §10 Option A/B); and HAR-RV-CJ at
  **monthly** frequency using daily returns within the month for the bipower/jump split.
- **What NOT to build:** HAR-RV-J at daily frequency — BPV is **identically zero** at $M=1$. Do not waste time
  on it, and if you have already half-built it, delete it.
- **Be scrupulous about labelling.** The HARQ adaptation is *not* Bollerslev–Patton–Quaedvlieg HARQ. Say so in
  one sentence with the citation. This costs you nothing and protects you completely.
- **Calibrate expectations:** HAR-RV-CJ's jump component is weakly persistent by the original authors' own
  finding, so expect a small gain. The YZ-proxy HAR is where the real improvement is.

### 3. Model Confidence Set — item 2
**Build third. Cheap once you have multiple models, and it is what makes your model comparisons publishable.**

- **Why third:** You now have a *set* of candidate models (HAR, HAR-YZ, HARQ-adapted, HAR-RV-CJ, RW, constant)
  and no principled way to say which differences are real. MCS is the answer, and it is what upgrades "HAR has
  lower QLIKE" into "HAR-YZ is in the 95% MCS and HAR is not."
- **Why it is cheap for you:** It is pure index arithmetic on a precomputed loss-differential matrix — **no
  linear algebra whatsoever**. You already have block bootstrap, so you already have the resampling machinery;
  MCS reuses it with a different statistic. $m = 6$ models gives 15 differential series.
- **Highest-leverage output:** the **MCS p-value per model**. One number per model, no significance-star
  theatre, and the MCS at any α is a threshold on it.
- **Gotcha to nail:** centre the bootstrap statistic on $\bar d_{ij}$ (not zero), and **reuse the same
  resample indices across all elimination steps**. Those are the two errors that silently produce wrong
  p-values.

### 4. Ledoit–Wolf constant-correlation shrinkage — item 4a
**Build fourth. It is the entry ticket to any portfolio construction.**

- **Why fourth:** Items 5 and 6 (HRP, vol-managed) both consume a covariance or correlation matrix. Shrinkage
  is what makes that matrix trustworthy. And it is the *only* dependency-free way to get a well-conditioned
  covariance estimate without touching eigenvalues.
- **Effort is genuinely modest.** It is $O(N^2 T)$ scalar accumulation — three nested loops, no matrix
  inversion, no eigendecomposition, no numpy. For N = 22, T ≈ 2000 that is under 10⁶ operations. Cache the
  demeaned $T \times N$ matrix once and every $\hat\pi$, $\hat\vartheta$, $s_{ij}$ is a column pass.
- **Two free correctness checks:** $\hat\rho$ must reduce to $\sum \hat\pi_{ii}$ when you zero the
  off-diagonal terms; and $\hat\delta$ should land in the 0.01–0.2 range for daily equity data. If
  $\hat\delta \approx 1$, your $\hat\gamma$ is wrong.
- **Skip 4b (analytical nonlinear shrinkage).** Cite it as related work, implement 4a. At $N/T \approx 0.01$
  the scalar shrinkage captures essentially all the benefit, and the Hilbert-transform machinery is a
  multi-week project that will fail numerically.

### 5. Hierarchical Risk Parity — item 3
**Build fifth. The payoff for the tool's actual purpose.**

- **Why fifth:** This is the portfolio construction method that *fits your constraints*, and it is the reason
  to do item 4 at all.
- **Why it suits a no-numpy codebase better than anything else:** HRP **never inverts a covariance matrix**.
  There is no `solve`, no Cholesky, no eigenvalue decomposition. The entire algorithm is: a naive
  $O(N^3)$ single-linkage agglomerative loop on a $22 \times 22$ distance matrix (~10⁴ operations), a
  dendrogram traversal to get the ordering, and a recursive bisection with running weights. It is arguably
  *easier* in pure Python than in numpy, because there is no temptation to vectorize the wrong thing.
- **Pair it with item 4:** run HRP on the Ledoit–Wolf-shrunk correlation matrix (Stage 1) and shrunk
  covariance (Stage 3 `V(C)`). That two-line combination is the single most defensible portfolio
  construction stack available to you, and it is what I would ship.
- **Gotchas to nail, in order of likelihood:** (a) `d = sqrt(0.5*(1−ρ))` — do not drop the 0.5, and do not
  use `1−ρ`; (b) **single** linkage, not average (this is the algorithm, not a preference); (c) use
  **correlation** for the distance and **covariance** for `V(C)` — mixing these is the most common HRP bug;
  (d) bisection at the midpoint of the quasi-diagonal order, not at the largest gap; (e) deduplicate
  perfectly-correlated assets before Stage 1.
- **Recompute quarterly, not daily.** The tree is stable; recomputing it daily manufactures turnover and
  will make your backtest look worse for no reason.

### 6. Volatility-managed overlay — item 1
**Build sixth. Highest-profile result, but only ship it with the caveat attached.**

- **Why sixth, despite being your first-listed item:** it is the largest *behavioural* change to the tool
  (it introduces leverage and turnover) and the one with the most contested evidence. Ship it on top of a
  solid risk stack, not underneath one.
- **It is nearly free given items 1–4.** You already have HAR volatility forecasts and VaR machinery. The
  whole implementation is: $\tilde f_{t+1} = (c/\hat\sigma^2_t)\cdot f_{t+1}$, $c$ set ex post for equal
  unconditional volatility.
- **Two things you must do or the result is meaningless:**
  1. **Winsorize the $1/\hat\sigma^2$ weight** (1st/99th percentile) or cap gross leverage. On a
     low-volatility asset this will otherwise blow up your backtest. This is not optional.
  2. **Report the block-bootstrap CI on the Sharpe difference**, and report it for out-of-sample,
     recursively-estimated scaling decisions. Cederburg et al.'s entire critique is that the in-sample
     result does not survive this. If your result survives it on your data, that is a genuinely interesting
     finding; if it does not, you have replicated the literature honestly and can say so.
- **Present it as:** a **risk-management overlay with a documented in-sample alpha and contested OOS,
  net-of-cost benefit, applying primarily to risk assets.** Cite Moreira–Muir, Cederburg et al. (JFE — get
  the journal right), Barroso–Detzel, and DeMiguel et al. together. A tool that shows both sides of this is
  more credible than one that shows the 25% headline.
